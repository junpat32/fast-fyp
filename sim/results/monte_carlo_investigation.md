# T-15 investigation — 14 October 2026

Verdict: current M1 underestimates uncertainty and also overstates the typical
future cushion in these synthetic tests. The generator and forecast share some
definitions but do not represent the same predictive distribution. Three to six
months do not justify the present six-month 80% coverage claim. More simulation
paths do not solve the limited-history problem.

No production forecast, generator configuration, frozen file or O2 evaluation
was changed. Reproduce with `python -m sim.investigate_monte_carlo`; raw results
are in `monte_carlo_investigation.json`. Seeds, populations, horizons and ablations
were specified in the script before viewing results. All examined seeds are now
development/audit data, not a fresh final evaluation set.

## 1. Is resampling too narrow?

Yes. A separate controlled experiment uses 1,000 independent histories at each
length from N(0,1), predicts the sum of six new independent monthly observations,
and takes P10–P90 from 2,000 ordinary resampled paths. There are no regime shifts,
debts, seasonal effects, cash floor or shocks. This isolates estimation error.

| History length | Empirical resampling coverage | Predictive Student-t coverage |
|---|---:|---:|
| 3 | 39.8% | 79.3% |
| 6 | 56.0% | 81.2% |
| 12 | 66.6% | 78.4% |
| 24 | 73.4% | 81.6% |
| 60 | 77.2% | 79.1% |

Why: the engine treats a handful of observed months as the entire distribution.
Repeating draws captures variation between those months but not uncertainty in
the estimated average or distribution. Income extremes absent from the history
cannot be generated by ordinary resampling. Employee income is more restrictive:
the median of the last three months is constant across every future path.

For iid months with variance sigma², predicting an H-month sum around H times
the estimated historical mean has error variance sigma²(H + H²/n). Ordinary
resampling represents H times the empirical variance and omits the H²/n term.
The empirical variance also averages only (n-1)/n of the population variance.
The unknown variance adds uncertainty too. The comparison above uses the
Student-t predictive formula for normal observations, scaled from mean to sum:
H*xbar ± t(0.9,n-1)*s*sqrt(H+H²/n).
[NIST prediction limits](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/software/dataplot/refman1/auxillar/predlimi.htm)
gives the corresponding future-mean formula and its normality assumptions.
This is evidence for including estimation uncertainty, not proof that normal
cash-flow forecasts or this formula will calibrate FAST's nonlinear cushion.

## 2. Generator versus forecast

| Component | Finding |
|---|---|
| Routine income | Generator has monthly noise even for employees; forecast freezes employee salary. Gig/student forecasts sample only observed values. |
| Income regimes | Generator has unemployment spells, rises and gig slow months. Forecast excludes these by D-14. These omissions create model mismatch by design. |
| Shocks | Same deck, uniform costs and eligibility multipliers. Generator additionally clusters events; forecast draws them independently. Frequencies remain assumptions. |
| Hidden cost | Generator medium noise subtracts a positive unlogged cost; forecast does not. This creates an expected cash-flow bias. |
| Debt and BNPL | Generator changes balances and instalments. Forecast freezes the historical median payment, subtracts sampled BNPL-funded purchases but does not schedule their future instalments. A stable median can approximate ongoing borrowing, but it does not conserve each plan's cash flows. |
| Calendar | Forecast divides past festive spending by 1.3 despite the unknown realised factor being 1.2–1.4. More seriously, it applies the student semester factor to ALL essentials; generator applies it only to education plus discretionary spending. This is an implementation mismatch. |
| Behaviour and serial dependence | Generator can reduce spending for three months after financial pressure. Independent monthly resampling loses that persistence and retains accidental income/spending correlation estimated from very few rows. |
| Cash and credit | Both floor cash at zero. Generator can draw credit and later pay a card balance in full; forecast has neither mechanism. Shortfall probability is therefore not precisely the same event for cardholders. |
| Cushion | Both use cash divided by rolling three-month essentials plus debt commitments. That denominator omits separately recorded family support in both implementations: internally consistent, but incomplete as a household needs measure. Changing it requires a design decision. |

World ablations use 120 users, seed 20261015, six observed months and 2,000 paths.
They change only in-memory diagnostic generator settings; each forecast still
uses observed history and the unchanged production model. Because ablations alter
history and downstream behaviour too, their effects are not additive causal
decompositions of the original coverage gap.

| Diagnostic world | Terminal coverage | Median forecast minus truth (cushion months) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Full generator | 53.3% | +0.556 |
| No hidden costs | 50.0% | +0.274 |
| No income regimes | 62.5% | +0.554 |
| No shock clustering | 54.2% | +0.556 |
| All three removed | 63.3% | +0.224 |

Removing hidden costs reduces median bias but does not improve coverage in this
sample. Removing regimes helps, yet the simplified world still misses 80%.
Therefore neither a single shock setting nor hidden costs explain the whole gap.
Two additional full-world seeds give 58.3% and 60.8% (120 users each); combined
with the original diagnostic seed, 207/360 = 57.5%. Repeated undercoverage makes
the earlier failure unlikely to be merely one unlucky simulation seed.

## 3. Is 3–6 months enough?

The first audit compared history months 1–3 forecasting month 9 with history
months 1–6 forecasting month 12. It was not a fair comparison of history length.
The investigation fixes the origin at month 6 and forecasts month 12 in both cases:

| Inputs/settings | Terminal coverage | Mean interval width (cushion months) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Last 3 observations, 2,000 paths | 41.7% | 2.431 |
| Last 6 observations, 2,000 paths | 53.3% | 2.654 |
| Last 6 observations, 500 paths | 49.2% | 2.660 |

The new results use forecast seeds 50000+user_id; they do not replace the original
51.7% audit. Path-count changes alter Monte Carlo draws; the similar interval
widths show why increasing paths is not a remedy for model uncertainty.

With six observations, coverage at horizons 1, 3 and 6 is 68.3%, 67.5%, 53.3%.
A shorter horizon helps here but still does not automatically deliver 80%.
Three months can support an illustrative scenario, but does not reliably estimate
tail behaviour, seasonality or rare events. Six months helps; there is no universal
history length that guarantees calibration. The production model currently
discards all but six months, so supplying a year alone would change nothing.

The current `test_calibration_reports_actual_coverage` asserts sample count only.
Its green status confirms execution, not acceptable calibration. The initial
claim that all outputs were ready for review needed this distinction.

## 4. Changes to propose, not silently apply

| Option | Description | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| A — recommended | Correct observable accounting/calendar mismatches; then add uncertainty about the user's estimated income/spending parameters, and retain modest employee pay variation. Specify model and assumptions before a fresh evaluation. | Addresses both bias and narrow ranges. Distributional assumptions and short histories still limit reliability. Needs Patrick's model choice and D-79 amendment. |
| B | Retain empirical sampling but allow longer histories, account for persistence, and show shorter-horizon projections until enough data accumulates. | Fewer distributional assumptions, but slow onboarding and longer history does not remove missing regimes or parameter uncertainty by itself. |
| C | Keep current mechanics as explicitly conditional scenarios with no 80% coverage claim. | Smallest change; does not fulfil the calibration ambition of T-15. |

For A, candidates include a small parametric predictive model or a carefully
specified two-stage resampling model with parameter uncertainty. A naive second
bootstrap from just three points is not guaranteed to repair coverage. Neither
candidate should simply multiply widths until this dataset reaches 80%.
Adding unemployment regimes would require Patrick to revisit D-14; it must not
be slipped into an implementation fix. More detailed debt schedules require
actual schedule inputs; APR alone is also inadequate for modelling all Malaysian
loan contracts and early-settlement rules.

Fair evaluation protocol: treat all used users/seeds as development data; record
the revised model, constants, noise levels, forecast origin, evaluation population
and acceptance criteria before running a disjoint final audit. Report coverage,
below/above misses, interval width and an interval score, median bias and
employment-group results, plus the shortfall probability's separate calibration.
Keep failures visible. P10/P90 cushion coverage does not validate shortfall risk,
CFaR or Expected Shortfall. Any fitted calibration layer needs a separate
calibration population and a fresh test population and would change the current
own-history-only scope; Patrick must approve that explicitly.

Confidence: high that M1 omits material estimation uncertainty and the identified
accounting mismatches exist. Below 80% confidence that any proposed replacement
will achieve 80% coverage until it passes a new evaluation. The ablations use one
seed and small employment groups; do not interpret them as precise contributions.
